# Your AI Habit Has Three Bills: Power, Water, and Your Brain

Every time you ask a chatbot to write an email, summarise a document, or explain something you could have looked up yourself, three meters start running. One measures electricity. One measures water. One measures how much thinking you just outsourced — and whether you will get it back.

None of this is a reason to stop using AI. It is a reason to know what it costs, because the costs are real, they are growing fast, and almost none of them show up on any bill you receive. They show up on everyone else's.

## The short answer

AI runs on data centers. In 2024, the world's data centers used about 415 terawatt-hours of electricity — roughly 1.5% of everything humanity generated. The International Energy Agency expects that to roughly double to about 945 terawatt-hours by 2030, more than Japan uses. AI is driving almost all of the growth: AI-focused data centers saw their electricity use surge about 50% in 2025 alone.

Water is the quieter bill. A data center cooled by evaporating water consumes roughly one litre for every kilowatt-hour its computers use. US data centers directly consumed about 17.4 billion gallons of water in 2023 — about as much as 160,000 American households — and that could reach 38 to 73 billion gallons by 2028. The power plants feeding those data centers use far more water still.

And then there is the third bill, the one nobody invoices you for. When you hand a thinking task to AI, your brain does less of it. A 2025 MIT study found ChatGPT users showed up to 55% reduced brain connectivity compared with people who wrote unaided, and 83% of them could not quote a single passage from essays they had just written. A separate randomised trial found the ChatGPT group scored 58% on a retention test 45 days later, against 69% for people who studied without AI. The skill does not vanish overnight. It atrophies, the way a muscle does when you stop using it — which is reversible, but only if you notice it happening.

## Bill one: electricity, or why your power grid is now a chatbot's landlord

Here is the number that matters. In 2024, global data centers used about 415 terawatt-hours of electricity. By 2030, the IEA's base case puts that at about 945 terawatt-hours — more than Japan, a country of 125 million people, consumes in a year. Gartner puts total data center electricity at about 565 terawatt-hours in 2026 alone, a 26% jump in a single year, with peak demand hitting 132 gigawatts.

AI is not a small slice of that growth. It is nearly all of it. Gartner's June 2026 forecast has AI-optimised servers using about 175 terawatt-hours in 2026 — up 84% from roughly 95 terawatt-hours in 2025 — and making up about 31% of all data center power. The IEA expects AI's share of data center electricity to roughly triple between 2025 and 2030. In the United States, Goldman Sachs puts data center power demand at 31 gigawatts in 2025, climbing to 41 gigawatts in 2026. A single AI-optimised rack built for NVIDIA's H100 or Blackwell GPUs draws 60 kilowatts or more. That is roughly what sixty kettles draw, running all day, every day, in one metal box.

The twist is that most of this electricity is not going to training the giant models you hear about. It is going to inference — answering your prompts. In 2023, inference was about a third of all AI computing. By 2026 it is 80 to 90%. Every "write me a poem about my cat" and "summarise this 40-page PDF" is a tiny withdrawal from a grid that was not built for a billion people to have a tireless intern on tap.

So who pays? You do, eventually, just not on a line item labelled "chatbot." When data center demand spikes in a region, the cost of building new generation and transmission gets spread across every ratepayer in that grid. Your electricity bill goes up because the grid had to grow to feed the machines that answer your questions for free. The service is free. The infrastructure is not. The invoice arrives addressed to everyone.

The joke, if you want one, is that we spent decades being told to turn off the lights when we leave a room, and then we built a machine that leaves the lights on for the entire planet so it can write our emails for us.

## Bill two: water, or the part nobody photographs

Electricity gets the headlines. Water gets the droughts.

A data center is a warehouse full of computers that never sleep, and every processor inside generates heat. The cheapest way to remove a lot of heat is to evaporate water. A data center cooled this way consumes roughly one litre of water for every kilowatt-hour its computers use — about 1,100 litres per megawatt-hour, or about 2.6 million gallons per megawatt per year, by NVIDIA's figure. A 100-megawatt AI campus running flat out on evaporative cooling would drink roughly 700,000 gallons a day. Google's largest US campus, in Council Bluffs, Iowa, has consumed about 2.8 million gallons a day on average.

Add it up and the numbers get hard to picture. US data centers directly consumed about 17.4 billion gallons of water in 2023, according to Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory — about 48 million gallons a day, roughly what 160,000 American households use. That could reach 38 to 73 billion gallons by 2028. And that is only the direct use. The power plants that generate the electricity consume far more water cooling themselves: Berkeley Lab put the indirect footprint of US data centers at nearly 800 billion litres in 2023, against 66 billion litres directly. The water you never see is an order of magnitude larger than the water you do.

Per prompt, the amount is almost comically small. Google reported its median Gemini text prompt uses 0.24 watt-hours of energy and 0.26 millilitres of water — about five drops. Five drops! You could spill more than that filling a glass. The problem was never one prompt. The problem is a billion people each using five drops, plus the millions of gallons a day the buildings need just to exist, plus the power plants behind them, all drawing from the same rivers and aquifers as the farms and towns next door. The strain is local, which is why it is invisible from anywhere else.

There is a genuine fix on the way, and it is worth naming honestly. Microsoft says all its new data center designs since August 2024 use closed-loop, zero-evaporation cooling, and its fleet averaged 0.30 litres per kilowatt-hour in 2024 — far below the roughly 1 litre industry figure. Closed-loop systems consume close to zero water on site, but they use more electricity instead. It is a trade-off, not a free win: you can pay the water bill or the power bill, but the heat has to go somewhere. The laws of thermodynamics do not take bribes.

## Bill three: your brain, or the subscription you did not know you signed

This is the bill that does not arrive in the mail, because there is no meter for it. But the research is now specific enough to be uncomfortable.

In 2025, researchers at the MIT Media Lab split 54 participants into three groups and had them write essays over several months, monitoring brain activity with EEG across 32 regions. One group used ChatGPT, one used a search engine, one used nothing. The ChatGPT group showed the weakest neural connectivity — up to 55% reduced compared with the brain-only group — and their engagement declined over repeated sessions. In a final session, people who switched from ChatGPT to writing unaided showed weaker engagement and weaker recall, a lingering effect the authors called "cognitive debt." And 83% of the ChatGPT users could not quote a single passage from essays they had just written. They had produced the words, but the words had not passed through them.

That study is a preprint with a small sample — 18 people per group for the neural monitoring — and the lead researcher has pushed back on coverage that framed it as proof of brain damage. Fair enough. But it is not alone. A preregistered randomised controlled trial from Brazil (Fundação Getulio Vargas and UFRJ, 2025) tracked 120 participants through a learning task and a surprise retention test 45 days later. The ChatGPT group scored 58%. The group that studied without AI scored 69%. The 11-point gap did not narrow for people with more prior AI experience. When AI does the synthesis, the brain encodes the material less deeply, and the loss shows up weeks later, not immediately.

A 2025 survey of 666 people (Gerlich, Societies) found a significant negative correlation between frequent AI tool use and critical thinking scores, and the relationship ran largely through cognitive offloading — the mechanism, not a coincidence. Participants aged 17 to 25 relied on AI the most and scored the lowest. And Microsoft Research with Carnegie Mellon, surveying 319 knowledge workers at CHI 2025, found that workers with more confidence in AI reported less critical thinking, while workers with more confidence in their own abilities reported more. Seventy percent said they used less cognitive effort for reading comprehension when ChatGPT was available.

None of this means AI makes you stupid. It means something more precise and more useful: AI makes you worse at the specific cognitive tasks you hand over to it. Memory retention, critical synthesis, independent reasoning — the things you stop practicing. The mechanism has a name, cognitive offloading, and it is not new. We have been doing it with GPS and calculators for years. What is new is the category of tasks being handed over. A calculator offloads arithmetic. A chatbot offloads reasoning, writing, and judgement. That is a much larger share of what we used to call thinking.

The honest caveat, because the research deserves it: none of this is brain damage, and the evidence suggests the effect is reversible with practice. It is skill atrophy, not injury. A muscle you stop using gets weaker; it does not disappear. But a muscle you stop using also does not warn you. It just quietly gets weaker, and one day you try to lift something and discover you cannot. The danger is not that AI ruins your brain. The danger is that you do not notice the subscription renewing.

## So what do you actually do with this?

You do not delete the apps. That would be like refusing to use a car because roads cost money. You use them like a tool that has a price, instead of like a free lunch.

A few rules that cost nothing and keep all three bills down:

- **Use AI for the draft, not the thinking.** Let it produce a first version, then rewrite the key parts yourself. The encoding happens when you do the synthesis, not when you read the output. If you never touch the words, you never learn them.
- **Ask it to explain, not just answer.** "Why is this the answer?" forces your brain to follow the reasoning instead of just copying the conclusion. It is the difference between hiring a tutor and hiring a ghostwriter.
- **Do one hard thing a day without it.** Write a paragraph, solve a problem, summarise an article from memory. It is the cognitive equivalent of taking the stairs. Boring, effective, and nobody will applaud you for it.
- **Notice when you reach for it out of reflex.** If you catch yourself asking the chatbot something you already know, or could figure out in thirty seconds, that is the offloading habit running on autopilot. Pause. Thirty seconds of thinking is cheaper than a habit you cannot switch off.

The future is not a choice between using AI and not using it. That ship sailed, hit an iceberg made of GPUs, and sank. The choice is whether you use it as a bicycle for the mind — something that makes you faster while you still pedal — or as a wheelchair you forget how to stand up from. The bicycle and the wheelchair look similar from the outside. The difference is whether your legs still work when you get off.

## FAQ

**Is one chatbot prompt really using that much electricity and water?**
No. A single median text prompt uses about 0.24 watt-hours and about 0.26 millilitres of water — five drops. The cost is aggregate: billions of prompts, plus the data centers and power plants that have to exist to serve them at any hour.

**Will AI make my electricity bill go up?**
Not as a line item. But when data center demand forces new generation and transmission in your region, those costs are spread across ratepayers. Your bill rises because the grid grew to feed the machines, not because a chatbot sent you an invoice.

**Is the water problem being fixed?**
Partly. Closed-loop cooling uses close to zero water on site, and Microsoft's new designs use it. But closed-loop uses more electricity instead. The heat has to go somewhere — you trade a water bill for a power bill.

**Does using ChatGPT actually make me dumber?**
No — not in the clinical sense, and the effect looks reversible. What the research shows is narrower: you get worse at the specific tasks you hand over (memory, synthesis, independent reasoning), because you practice them less. It is skill atrophy from disuse, not damage.

**What is "cognitive debt"?**
The MIT Media Lab's term for the lingering effect: after sustained AI-assisted writing, people who switched back to writing unaided showed weaker neural engagement and weaker recall. The debt accumulates with use and fades with practice.

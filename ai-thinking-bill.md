# The Thinking Bill: What Happens When You Stop Doing the Hard Part

There is a quiet deal most of us have made with AI, and we made it without reading the terms. The deal is: the machine does the thinking, and we keep the credit. We write the email, or rather, it writes the email, and we sign our name. We solve the problem, or rather, it solves the problem, and we paste the answer. We feel productive. We feel smart. And somewhere, unnoticed, the part of us that used to do the actual thinking gets a little quieter.

This is not a moral panic. It is a measurement. Researchers have now put numbers on what happens to your brain when you hand the hard part to a chatbot, and the numbers are specific enough to be useful — and uncomfortable enough to be worth your attention.

## The short answer

When AI handles a cognitive task, your brain does less of it. That reduced effort costs you something later: weaker memory of what you produced, weaker recall weeks after learning, and lower critical thinking scores. The mechanism is called cognitive offloading, and it is not new — we have been doing it with GPS and calculators for years. What is new is how much of our thinking we are handing over, and how early it starts.

The effect is not brain damage. It is skill atrophy from disuse, and the evidence suggests it is reversible. But it is also silent. You do not feel your reasoning getting weaker the way you feel a muscle getting sore. You just notice, one day, that you cannot do something you used to be able to do — and you reach for the chatbot to do it for you, which is exactly the habit that got you there.

## What the MIT study actually found

The study that started the conversation came from the MIT Media Lab in 2025. Nataliya Kosmyna and colleagues split 54 participants into three groups and had them write essays over several months, monitoring brain activity with EEG across 32 regions. One group used ChatGPT. One used a search engine. One used nothing.

The ChatGPT group showed the weakest neural connectivity of the three — up to 55% reduced compared with the brain-only group — and their engagement declined over repeated sessions. In a final session, people who switched from ChatGPT to writing unaided showed weaker engagement and weaker recall. The authors called this lingering effect "cognitive debt." And 83% of the ChatGPT users could not quote a single passage from essays they had just written.

Read that again. They had produced the words. The words had not passed through them.

Now, the caveats, because they matter. This is a preprint. It has not been peer-reviewed. The sample is small — 18 people per group for the neural monitoring — and other researchers have published a detailed statistical commentary flagging inconsistencies. Kosmyna herself has pushed back on coverage that framed the result as proof of brain damage. That is fair. But the study is not the whole story. It is one data point in a pattern that is now showing up across several independent studies, with different methods, different populations, and the same direction.

## The retention gap

The strongest single piece of evidence is not the MIT study. It is a preregistered randomised controlled trial from Brazil — Fundação Getulio Vargas and UFRJ, 2025 — because preregistration means the researchers declared their hypothesis and methods before collecting data, which makes it much harder to fish for a flattering result.

They tracked 120 participants through a learning task, then gave them a surprise retention test 45 days later. The group that used ChatGPT scored 58%. The group that studied without AI scored 69%. An 11-point gap, appearing six weeks after the learning happened. And the gap did not narrow for participants with more prior AI experience. Being good at using the tool did not protect you. The researchers attributed the gap to weaker initial encoding: when AI does the synthesis, the brain processes the material less deeply, and you remember less of it later.

Think about what that means in practice. You ask the chatbot to summarise a report. You read the summary. You feel like you understand the report. Six weeks later, someone asks you about it, and you remember almost nothing — because you never actually processed it. The chatbot did. You just watched.

## The critical thinking correlation

A 2025 study in the journal Societies (Gerlich) surveyed and interviewed 666 people and found a significant negative correlation between frequent AI tool use and critical thinking scores. The relationship ran largely through cognitive offloading: the more people let AI do the evaluating, the less practiced their own evaluation became. Participants aged 17 to 25 relied on AI the most and scored the lowest. Gerlich is careful to point out that the study shows correlation, not causation — it is possible that people who think less critically are more drawn to AI, rather than AI causing the decline. But the mechanism (offloading) is the same one the other studies identify, and the age pattern is the one you would expect if the tool were doing the damage.

Microsoft Research and Carnegie Mellon reached a related conclusion at the CHI 2025 conference. Hao-Ping Lee and colleagues surveyed 319 knowledge workers who use generative AI at least weekly and gathered 936 examples of real work tasks. Workers with more confidence in AI reported less critical thinking. Workers with more confidence in their own abilities reported more. Seventy percent said they used less cognitive effort for reading comprehension when ChatGPT was available. The pattern is not that AI users are lazy. It is that confidence in the tool quietly replaces the effort of doing the work yourself — and the effort was doing something important.

A University of Technology Sydney report from March 2026 reviewed this literature and named the distinction that matters most: not all offloading is equal. Offloading a routine step (a calculation, a lookup) frees your mind for harder work. Offloading the hard part itself (the synthesis, the judgement, the reasoning) removes the very practice that keeps those skills sharp. The difference is whether you are using AI as a lever or as a replacement.

## Why this is not like GPS

People who defend heavy AI use often point out that we have been offloading cognition for decades. We use GPS instead of navigating. We use calculators instead of doing long division. We use spellcheck instead of memorising spelling. And we did not become helpless. We became more capable, because we spent the freed-up effort on harder problems.

That argument is half right. GPS is a good analogy — and it is also a warning. A 2020 study from McGill University measured the lifetime GPS use of 50 regular drivers and tested their spatial memory. The heavier the GPS use, the worse the spatial memory. People who navigated without GPS built richer mental maps. People who followed turn-by-turn directions did not. The skill did not vanish, but it got weaker, and the people who needed it most — the ones navigating unfamiliar places — were the ones who had practiced it least.

The difference with AI is scale and scope. A calculator offloads arithmetic. GPS offloads navigation. A chatbot offloads reasoning, writing, synthesis, and judgement — the core of what we used to call thinking. That is not one skill. That is the whole toolkit. And unlike GPS, which you use when you are going somewhere new, a chatbot is available for everything, all the time, which means the temptation to offload is constant.

## The part nobody wants to say out loud

Here is the uncomfortable bit. The people most at risk are not the ones who use AI badly. They are the ones who use it well.

If you are a skilled user, you get better outputs. The chatbot writes a cleaner draft, a sharper summary, a more polished email. The output is so good that there is no reason to touch it. You read it, you approve it, you send it. You did not write a word. You did not struggle with a sentence. You did not notice the gap in your own reasoning, because the chatbot filled it before you could see it.

The MIT study's most telling detail is not the brain connectivity number. It is that 83% of ChatGPT users could not quote their own essays. They had not written them in any meaningful sense. They had supervised a machine writing them. And supervision is not the same as authorship. It does not build the same skill, it does not leave the same memory, and it does not teach you anything about the subject.

This is why "cognitive debt" is such a good name for it. Debt is not a sudden catastrophe. It is a slow accumulation. Each time you let the machine do the synthesis, you borrow a little against your own ability to synthesise. The interest is invisible until the day you need the skill and discover the account is empty.

## How to use AI without paying the thinking bill

You do not have to quit. Quitting would be like refusing to use a car because you might forget how to walk. The goal is to use the tool in a way that keeps the muscle.

**Make the AI explain, not just answer.** When it gives you a result, ask "why is this the answer?" and "what would change your mind?" That forces your brain to follow the reasoning instead of just copying the conclusion. It is the difference between hiring a tutor and hiring a ghostwriter. A tutor makes you smarter. A ghostwriter makes you look smarter.

**Do the first draft yourself, even a bad one.** Write a rough paragraph, a messy outline, a half-formed argument — then let the AI improve it. The encoding happens when you do the synthesis. If your first contact with the idea is the AI's polished version, you never wrestle with it, and the wrestling is the learning.

**Use AI to check your work, not to do your work.** Write the email, then ask the AI to critique it. Solve the problem, then ask the AI if your reasoning holds up. This flips the dynamic: you are the author, the AI is the editor. Editors make you better. Ghostwriters make you dependent.

**Do one hard thing a day without it.** A paragraph from memory. A problem solved by hand. An article summarised without asking for a summary. It is the cognitive equivalent of taking the stairs. Boring, effective, and nobody will applaud you for it. That is the point. You are not doing it for applause. You are doing it so the muscle still works when you need it.

**Notice the reflex.** If you catch yourself reaching for the chatbot for something you already know, or could figure out in thirty seconds, pause. That is the habit running on autopilot. Thirty seconds of thinking is cheaper than a habit you cannot switch off.

## The real question

The question is not whether AI makes us dumber. The research says it does not — not in the clinical sense, and the effect looks reversible. The question is whether we will notice the trade we are making, and whether we will make it deliberately.

A bicycle makes you faster while you still pedal. A wheelchair carries you while your legs atrophy. From the outside, they look similar — both get you where you are going, both feel like progress. The difference is whether your legs still work when you get off.

AI is a bicycle. But only if you keep pedalling.
